Say no clearly

Try “No,” “No, thank you,” or “I’m not able to do that.” You do not need an excuse. If you are genuinely undecided, ask for time before answering.

Acknowledge+ clear no+ optional alternative
Stay on message

Use the broken-record technique

Choose one short sentence. When the other person distracts, pressures, questions, or insults, calmly return to it. Avoid adding new reasons that can become new debate material.

Care without giving in

Acknowledge and hold your position

Showing that you heard disappointment or hurt is not the same as agreeing or changing your answer.

Check before reacting

Handle criticism

If it is true

Own the part that fits. Mistakes are human; repair or apologize when appropriate.

If it is not true

Say so clearly. Do not counterattack. Repeat your position and disengage from insults.

If it is partly true

Agree with the accurate part, correct the rest, and ask for a specific change.

Which communication style shows up?

No style is a permanent identity. Notice the pattern, then practice a different response.

Passive

My needs disappear

I prioritize other people’s needs, wants, or feelings at my own expense.

  • May be quiet or soft-spoken
  • May avoid expressing needs
  • May look down or away
  • May struggle to stand up for myself
Aggressive

Only my needs matter

I push my needs forward while the other person’s needs or rights get ignored.

  • Loud or overbearing delivery
  • Criticism, humiliation, or domination
  • Interrupting or not listening
  • Little willingness to compromise
Assertive

Both sets of needs matter

I stand up for my needs while listening to and respecting the other person.

  • Clear needs and wants
  • Steady tone and confident body language
  • Listens without interruption
  • Open to compromise

Assertiveness basics

  • Respect yourself: your needs, wants, and rights matter too.
  • Express thoughts and feelings calmly and factually; “I feel…” can help take ownership of your emotions.
  • Plan important conversations and prepare specific words.
  • It is okay to say “no” clearly without inventing a reason. You can offer to explore another solution.

Before you respond: make a U-turn

When emotion surges, create a little space before acting.

Pause
Create space between the trigger and your response.

Three questions

1
What am I feeling?
2
What do I need?
3
What response fits my values?

Recognize the trigger

Notice the surge: anger, frustration, defensiveness, anxiety, or another strong reaction. What do you notice in your body and mind?

Turn inward

Allow the emotion to be present rather than suppressing or judging it. Ask: What need or desire might be unmet? What is this emotion telling me?

Respond thoughtfully

Choose a response that considers both your needs and the other person’s needs. That might mean expressing a feeling, setting a boundary, or asking for support.

D

Describe

Describe the situation using observable facts. Avoid judgments. Ask yourself: “What would a camera have seen or heard?”

E

Express

Express your thoughts and feelings. “I” statements can help communicate your experience without blaming.

A

Assert

Say exactly what you want, simply and clearly. Make the request specific.

R

Reinforce

Explain the benefit of meeting the request: what does the other person gain?

M

Mindful

Notice posture, tone, facial expression, discomfort, and emotional vulnerability. Aim for a calm, respectful, willing posture.

A

Appear confident

Use clear speech, appropriate eye contact, an open posture, and a steady tone. Listen attentively when the other person responds.

N

Negotiate

Stay open and flexible. Prepare alternatives when the exact request cannot be met.

GIVE FAST: protect the relationship and your self-respect

These skills help you communicate with respect while staying connected to yourself.

G

Gentle

Be respectful, kind, and courteous. Avoid personal judgments. When responding to another point, “and” can be less dismissive than “but.”

I

Interested

Use eye contact, nodding, no interruption, and reflections of the main points to show you are listening.

V

Validate

Acknowledge the other person’s feelings. Validation means recognizing their experience; it does not require agreeing with their thoughts or emotions.

E

Easy manner

Use a flexible, calm demeanor. Relax your body and communicate without unnecessary tension.

F

Fair

Be fair and objective. Consider the other person’s wants and needs as well as your own. Check the facts.

A

Apology-free

Do not apologize simply for asserting yourself, making a request, having feelings, or holding a different point of view.

S

Stick to values

Know your values, ethics, and morals. Do not abandon them just to avoid conflict or appease someone.

T

Truthful

Stay with the facts. Avoid exaggeration, embellishment, or judgmental labels.

Reflective listening

Show the other person that you heard the meaning, not just the words.

A simple formula

Reflect the main point in your own words. Use a tone that leaves room for correction: “I think this is what you’re telling me—correct me if I’m wrong.”

  • “I hear you saying that…”
  • “It sounds like you feel…”
  • “You’re telling me that…”

Try to reflect emotions, including emotions you infer from tone or body language. Your reflection does not have to be perfect; being corrected can help you understand better. Focus on the main point rather than every detail.

One-sentence assertive formula

Fact + feeling/impact + clear request + possible solution.

Example: “When the music is on late, I have trouble sleeping. I need quieter evenings. Could you use headphones or move the speakers?”
